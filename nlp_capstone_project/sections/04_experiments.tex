\section{Experiments}

This section defines the evaluation layout for PrismMathQA. The goal is to prepare a consistent reporting format for future model checkpoints trained with AVSD. As stated in the introduction, the report is restricted to mathematical reasoning datasets, so the benchmark suite contains GSM8K, AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25.

\subsection{Evaluation Setting}

The evaluation suite contains four math benchmarks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GSM8K}: grade-school arithmetic word problems that test multi-step numerical reasoning \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}.
    \item \textbf{AIME24}: American Invitational Mathematics Examination 2024, representing competition-style algebra, geometry, number theory, and combinatorics \citep{zhang2024aime}.
    \item \textbf{AIME25}: American Invitational Mathematics Examination 2025, used as a newer competition benchmark \citep{zhang2025aime}.
    \item \textbf{HMMT25}: Harvard-MIT Mathematics Tournament 2025, a difficult contest-style benchmark \citep{balunovic2025matharena}.
\end{itemize}

The default PrismMathQA reporting metric is \textbf{Avg@4}. For each problem, the model may produce four sampled attempts, and success is measured by whether the correct answer appears among those attempts. Avg@4 is appropriate for a tutoring-oriented reasoning model because it captures whether the model can reach a valid solution under moderate sampling without relying on a large number of attempts.

The compared training methods are evaluated on the same Qwen3-4B backbone. The \textbf{Base} row is the unfinetuned reasoning model, while \textbf{SFT} denotes supervised fine-tuning on static mathematical traces. \textbf{GRPO} uses verifiable final-answer rewards through group relative policy optimization \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}. \textbf{RLSD} refers to RLVR with Self-Distillation, which combines verifiable rewards with self-distillation signals for fine-grained update magnitude \citep{yang2026selfdistilledrlvr}. \textbf{OPSD} is the single-view on-policy self-distillation baseline \citep{zhao2026selfdistilled}, and \textbf{AVSD} extends this setting to four privileged views: full solution, partial rationale, final answer, and concise hint \citep{nguyen2026avsd}.

\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:math-results} compares finetuning methods under the same Qwen3-4B backbone, so the main variable is the training signal rather than model scale. RLSD obtains the best GSM8K and AIME24 scores, while AVSD remains second-best on both. GRPO improves over the base model on three of the four benchmarks, but the weaker SFT row suggests that static supervised traces alone are not sufficient for robust mathematical reasoning.

The strongest AVSD results appear on the harder contest-style benchmarks. AVSD is best on AIME25 and HMMT25, improving over the single-view OPSD baseline by $2.83$ points on AIME25 and $0.94$ points on HMMT25. This supports the core AVSD hypothesis: when problems require longer multi-step reasoning, aggregating several privileged teacher views can provide a more stable training target than relying on one view.

\subsection{Number of Privileged Views}

\midrule
1 view: full solution & 94.98 & 73.31 & 65.46 & 43.31 \\

\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:view-count} shows a consistent pattern: performance rises as the teacher family grows from one privileged view to four. Moving from the OPSD-style single-view setting to the full AVSD setting gives gains of $1.79$ on GSM8K, $2.89$ on AIME24, $2.83$ on AIME25, and $0.94$ on HMMT25. The main insight is that view diversity is most valuable on AIME-style multi-step contest problems, where full solutions, partial rationales, final answers, and hints provide complementary supervision rather than redundant signals.
